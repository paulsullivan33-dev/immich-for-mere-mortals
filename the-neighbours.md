# The Neighbours

**Scope:** the systems Immich is compared
with — what each one trades, and why
people land where they land. Everything
here is the shape of each project as it
presents itself publicly; no rival was
installed for this guide.

**Google Photos** is the neighbour Immich
is deliberately shaped like, and the one
most readers are leaving or hedging
against. It is superb: effortless backup,
the best search in the business, faces,
sharing, all of it, on Google's machines.
The costs are the known ones — your
photos on someone else's computers, under
terms that can change, in an account that
can be lost, with storage rented by the
month forever. Immich exists because its
author wanted the shape without the
landlord. Choosing between them is
choosing who does the work and who holds
the keys, not choosing between good and
bad software.

**iCloud Photos** is the same trade in
Apple's clothes, with one difference
worth naming: it is a *sync* service at
heart, keeping every device identical,
where Immich is a *backup and browse*
service with the server as the home.
Households deep in Apple hardware often
run both — iCloud for the phone's own
convenience, Immich as the copy nobody
can switch off.

**PhotoPrism** is the other long-standing
self-hosted option, and the comparison
people actually research. Where Immich
leads with phone backup and a
Google-Photos feel, PhotoPrism leads with
the existing folder: it indexes a photo
directory in place, is strong on
metadata and file formats including raw,
and its people features historically sat
partly behind a membership. Different
centre of gravity: archive first versus
phones first. Both are respectable;
households pick by which problem they
have (chapter 7's folder problem argues
one way, chapter 6's phone problem the
other).

**Nextcloud Photos** comes with the
Nextcloud suite — files, calendar,
contacts, and photos as one feature among
many, on infrastructure a household may
already run. Its photo experience is the
plainer one; its argument is consolidation:
one server, one login, everything
adequate. People choose it when photos
are the third priority, not the first.

**Ente** is the privacy maximalist's
answer: end-to-end encrypted photo
storage, offered hosted *and*
self-hostable, open source throughout.
The encryption is real and changes the
sums — the server cannot see your photos,
so server-side faces and meaning-search
of Immich's kind can't happen the same
way, and losing your password can mean
losing the library. For some households
that is exactly the point; for others it
is a bridge too far. It is the neighbour
to read about if chapter 9's biometrics
paragraph bothered you.

**Synology Photos** (and its rivals on
other NAS brands) is the appliance
answer: buy the box, the photo service is
a checkbox, somebody else maintains the
software. The trade is the mirror of
Immich's — less machinery visible, less
control held, and your library's future
tied to a vendor's product decisions.

And one non-neighbour, to close a common
confusion: **sync tools** like Syncthing
(this series has a guide) are not rivals
to any of the above. They move files
faithfully between machines; they do not
know what a face is. Many Immich installs
quietly use one as the off-site leg of
chapter 9's backup plan — neighbours in
the useful sense after all.
